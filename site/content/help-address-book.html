The address book is the lodge's member list, kept once and used everywhere. It belongs to the program, not to any single newsletter, so it is there next month too — and for whoever takes the secretary's chair after you.

Where your members come from

Most committees already keep the list in a spreadsheet, and most lodges can export one from their membership system. Bring it in rather than retyping it: People → Import from a file…. It reads spreadsheets saved as .xlsx and lists saved as .csv.

The import asks one question per piece of lodge information — Name: which column has it? — rather than showing a grid of headings and hoping. Each answer offers you the column letter, its heading and the first couple of values in it, so you recognise the data rather than decode a label. It guesses every answer first, and says so.

Then it shows you what it is about to do, in plain counts. Nothing is written until you press the button on this screen, and everything it cannot use is listed rather than quietly dropped.

What it copes with, so you do not have to

  • Excel dates. A birthday column is usually a real date cell: it stores 45123 and merely shows 7/4. Exported carelessly, that number is what lands in the file. TrestleBoard reads it back as a date.
  • The same man on several rows. Some membership systems write one row per report item, so a brother appears two or three times. Rows carrying the same lodge member number are understood as one man.
  • Spouses in the same sheet. A row marked as a spouse never becomes a member. Where it says whose wife she is, her details go onto his card — and the review screen tells you how many rows that was.
  • A father and a son. John Smith Jr. and John Smith Sr. are the same name to almost any matcher. TrestleBoard will not merge two men who carry different lodge member numbers, whatever their names do.
  • A spreadsheet with a pivot table in it. Membership systems often export one. Rather than telling you to repair a file that is not broken, TrestleBoard reads it a second, simpler way.
  • Children in the list. A row marked as somebody's child is not a member and is never added as one; it is reported on the review screen instead.
  • Three telephone columns and no plain one. If your file has Home, Mobile and Work but nothing called Phone, the newsletter's number is filled from the mobile — and counted as a change on the review screen, rather than done quietly.

Four rules the import will not break, whatever the file looks like: it never removes anybody; a column you did not map is left alone; an empty cell in a mapped column is left alone too; and two people are only ever merged on an exact match — anything close is a question, never an action.

Adding and correcting people

People → People… opens the list. Type three letters of a name in the box at the top and there they are; the form on the right corrects one detail without disturbing anything else.

The search box looks at more than the name. Any of these finds a brother: his lodge member number, any of his telephone numbers, his email address, his street or town, his office, or his wife's name.

What the book holds

The form is in two tabs. The first is what somebody opening the window in a hurry came for — name, birthday, telephone, email, office, degree, whether he is still a member, and which mailing lists he is on.

The second holds everything a lodge's own membership records usually carry: his lodge member number, the letters after his name, his postal address, whether post to it comes back undelivered, his home, mobile and work telephone numbers, his wife's name and how to reach her, and a box for notes.

A wife is fields on her husband's card, never a separate person in the list. That is not a preference, it is how it is built — there is no row for her, so she cannot appear in the birthday list, the officers table or a count of printed copies.

Birthdays, and the year

The newsletter prints a month and a day, and never a year. If your lodge's records carry a full birth date, TrestleBoard keeps the year — it is your data, and throwing it away would mean importing again to get it back — but nothing that reaches a printed page can see it.

What it saves you

  • The birthday list fills itself in. It knows which month the issue is for and takes the brethren whose birthdays fall in it — nobody who has passed, and nobody off the rolls.
  • The officers wizard offers names. Instead of asking you to spell a name it already knows, it shows you who it could be.
  • Telephone numbers stay in one place. Correct a number once and every list that prints it is offered the correction.
  • Sending it round knows who gets it how. The email goes to the brethren in Gets the newsletter by email, and you are told how many printed copies to run off for the rest.

Sending the list to somebody else

People → Save as a spreadsheet… writes the whole book out as an .xlsx file named for the day you made it. Every cell is written as text on purpose: a real date cell would print a birthday as 7/4/1900, and a numeric telephone number would come back as 8.03555E+09.

Export it, edit it in Excel, and bring it straight back: only the cells you changed move — including when what you changed was somebody's name. That is also how you share the list with another member of the committee, since the address book itself never leaves your computer.

Where it is kept, and who can see it

On your computer, in the program's own folder, and nowhere else. TrestleBoard has no account, no server and no synchronisation — nothing about your members leaves the machine you typed them into unless you send a newsletter, save a spreadsheet, or hand on a pack yourself.

A copy of the list is kept each time it changes — ten of them, rotating — so a mistaken import or a deletion can be undone. People → Undo the last change puts back what you had a moment ago; People → Restore an earlier version… lists the kept copies with their dates and how many people were in each.

And if the file cannot be read at all — a backup program holding it open, a permissions change — TrestleBoard says so and refuses to write over it, rather than quietly starting an empty list on top of your members.

Handing over to the next secretary

Pack everything up for whoever comes next… puts the address book, your saved templates, your standing paragraphs and your settings into a single file. The person taking over opens it with Bring in a pack…, which shows them exactly what it would replace before it replaces anything.
